# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Imbalanced fraud detection and evaluation

Credit-card fraud detection is a rare-event classification problem in which the positive class is small relative to legitimate activity. Practical fraud-detection settings are non-stationary, and confirmed labels may arrive after a delay [8]. These conditions motivate evaluation that reflects the intended use of the model.

The ULB/Kaggle dataset used here is valuable for reproducible benchmarking because it contains a public binary label and anonymized transaction variables. However, the PCA transformation removes direct semantic interpretation of `V1`–`V28`, and no payment-channel field identifies card-present or card-not-present transactions. Results from this dataset therefore support general credit-card transaction-fraud analysis, not a claim about CNP-only fraud.

ROC-AUC summarizes ranking across false-positive and true-positive rates [6]. When legitimate transactions dominate, even a small false-positive rate can produce a large number of alerts. Precision–recall analysis makes the fraction of alerts that are fraud visible and is sensitive to the prevalence of fraud [7]. The project reports scikit-learn average precision (AP), the sum of precision weighted by recall increments, as its PR-AUC; this differs from trapezoidal PR area [9]. Both AP and ROC-AUC describe rankings, rather than operational cost at a particular threshold. Report prevalence, precision, recall, false positives and the chosen threshold alongside them.

Class weighting adjusts the training loss to penalize classes differently; it changes the learning objective without generating transactions. Inverse-frequency weights are a baseline, not necessarily a measurement of real monetary costs. A cost-sensitive decision also requires justified false-positive and false-negative costs and a threshold selected on validation data. Weighting or resampling may affect score calibration, so scores should not automatically be treated as real-world fraud probabilities [1]. SMOTE creates synthetic minority examples [2]; compare it with weighting using the same splits and keep resampling inside each training fold. Validation and test distributions should preserve their observed prevalence.

## 2.2 Temporal validation and leakage control

Random cross-validation assumes that observations are exchangeable. That assumption is questionable when transaction order, delayed labels, customer behaviour, or attacker adaptation matter. Chronological splitting better reflects deployment: training examples precede validation examples, which precede the final evaluation period. Learned scalers and distribution statistics must be fitted on training data only. Resampling must also be applied only to training folds, because transforming or oversampling validation/test data changes the evaluation distribution.

Random splitting estimates performance on observations mixed across the available period; it does not establish performance on a later period and does not alone prove that a feature leaks a target. For example, the ULB study in [10] reports stratified 60/20/20 splits and training-only preprocessing and augmentation. Those precautions address cross-partition fitting, but the split does not establish future-period generalization. By contrast, Breskuvienė and Dzemyda [11] describe an earliest-80%/later-20% split. These different evaluation designs and any differences in preprocessing, prevalence, or metrics prevent direct comparison of reported scores as if they measured the same task. ULB covers only a short time period, so even its chronological split cannot establish long-term drift robustness.

Actual lookahead occurs when a feature or learned transformation uses information unavailable at the prediction time: for example, a centered rolling window includes later transactions, fitting a scaler before splitting uses held-out distribution statistics, or oversampling before splitting can share information across partitions. In time-series cross-validation, each scaler, feature selector and sampler must be fitted again inside the corresponding training fold. Model and threshold selection must use validation data; the final period must remain separate. Delayed labels and same-timestamp ordering also require explicit assumptions; time ordering alone does not solve them [8].

### Time features and preprocessing

A periodic time variable can be represented by `sin(2πt/P)` and `cos(2πt/P)`, preserving proximity across the cycle boundary. The project's `Time` is elapsed seconds, so its 24-hour encoding describes phase relative to the dataset start, not verified local clock hour. Backward rolling statistics must use only information available by the scoring instant, with a defined ordering for ties and a documented initialization policy. The offline feature code uses previous rows for the rolling z-score; its percentile includes the current transaction, whose amount is available at scoring time. Neither feature should use later rows.

RobustScaler centers using the training median and scales by a training quantile range, by default the interquartile range [12]. It reduces sensitivity of the fitted scale to outliers but does not remove outliers or guarantee improved model performance. The project fits separate amount and time scalers on its training partition in [prepare_creditcard.py](../src/fraud_detection/prepare_creditcard.py). For cross-validation, the corresponding fit must be confined to each fold's training portion as well.

The project sorts transactions by `Time`, removes exact duplicates, and uses chronological partitions. Replay has scoped rolling contexts, but the existing offline and service feature implementations have a documented parity limitation; see [Chapter 6](CHAPTER_6_DISCUSSION_LIMITATIONS.md). Consequently, chronological ordering alone does not establish that historical offline results reproduce served predictions. The evaluation partition was also exposed in earlier work and is not a pristine final test.

## 2.3 Metrics for extreme imbalance

Accuracy is weak evidence when legitimate transactions dominate. A classifier can obtain high accuracy while missing many fraud cases. ROC-AUC measures ranking across thresholds and remains useful for comparing discrimination, but it can appear optimistic when the negative class is very large. Precision-recall analysis focuses on the trade-off between detecting positives and generating false alerts. Average precision (AP), reported here as PR-AUC in the project tables, is therefore particularly informative for rare fraud.

Threshold-dependent measures remain essential for operations. Recall measures the proportion of fraud cases detected, while precision measures the proportion of alerts that are fraud. F1 summarizes precision and recall but does not encode the asymmetric operational cost of missed fraud and investigator workload. This study additionally reports false alarms per 1,000 legitimate transactions and selects thresholds on validation data rather than tuning against the final evaluation partition.

## 2.4 Class weighting and resampling

Class weighting changes the loss contribution of the minority class without synthesizing new examples. It is often attractive for tree and linear models because it preserves the observed feature distribution and is computationally simple. SMOTE generates synthetic minority examples by interpolating between minority neighbours [2]. It can improve minority representation, but synthetic examples may be less realistic when the minority class is heterogeneous or temporally structured. Borderline-SMOTE concentrates synthesis near difficult decision boundaries, which can help some classifiers but may also amplify ambiguous points.

The comparison must be performed fairly: weighting or resampling is fitted within the training data only, while validation and final evaluation remain at their natural prevalence. The project therefore treats class weighting, SMOTE, and Borderline-SMOTE as alternative training strategies, not as transformations applied to held-out transactions. Their value is judged with AP, ROC-AUC, threshold metrics, false alarms, and computational evidence rather than accuracy alone.

## 2.5 TreeSHAP explainability

Lundberg et al. describe TreeSHAP as an efficient method for computing Shapley-value explanations for tree ensembles [4]. A SHAP value describes the contribution of a feature to a model output relative to a baseline under the explainer's assumptions. This supports global rankings and local investigation of true-positive and false-positive cases. In this project, TreeSHAP is used for Random Forest and XGBoost artifacts and the dashboard displays signed local contributions.

SHAP explanations are explanations of model behaviour, not causal effects. The anonymized PCA components cannot be given domain meanings that the dataset does not provide. Correlated features can also distribute attribution in ways that depend on the explainer's conditional assumptions. Consequently, SHAP should support analyst investigation and model debugging, not be presented as proof that a transaction feature caused fraud.

Background selection changes the reference distribution and can change both cost and attributions. A candidate acceleration is to summarize training data with 50 representative observations, but `k=50` is a design choice rather than an established optimum. The project's benchmark uses K-means centroids and selects the nearest actual training row for each centroid; these are centroid-nearest representatives, not necessarily the optimal solution to a k-medoids objective. On 100 benchmark rows, Random Forest explanation P95 increased from 40.88 ms to 134.20 ms, while XGBoost decreased from 43.20 ms to 32.16 ms. Each model had two of its global top-three features in common across settings. These small local measurements compare explainer configurations and do not establish universal acceleration, per-transaction fidelity, or end-to-end API latency; see the [benchmark](../reports/explainability/creditcard_treeshap_latency_f1.json).

## 2.6 Ensembles and genetic search

Random Forest aggregates randomized decision trees, reducing reliance on any single tree [13]. XGBoost builds boosted trees sequentially with a regularized objective and scalable split learning [14]. LightGBM is another gradient-boosting framework designed for efficient tree learning, including gradient-based sampling and feature bundling [15]. Their relative accuracy, training cost, inference latency and explanation cost depend on the dataset, hyperparameters and hardware; none is automatically best for fraud.

A genetic algorithm (GA) represents candidate hyperparameters as a population, scores candidates, and uses selection, crossover and mutation to generate subsequent candidates. Ma et al. [16] evaluate GA tuning for ULB fraud detection with preprocessing and selection restricted to training data or folds and an original-prevalence test set. Their stratified split does not establish performance on a future chronological period. A fair comparison against random or other search needs the same model family, parameter space, chronological folds, optimization metric, number of fits or compute budget, and documented seeds. Repeated search can overfit validation; retain a later evaluation period outside model selection. The current project reports Random Forest and XGBoost comparisons; LightGBM and GA tuning are literature-based candidates, not completed project experiments.

## 2.7 Calibration and operational thresholds

Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed event frequencies. Isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing, potentially stepwise mapping from scores to observed outcomes; it is flexible but can overfit when calibration data are scarce and may introduce score ties [17]. Fraud prevalence makes the number of positive examples particularly important. Fit the model on earlier data, fit any calibrator on separate later predictions at natural prevalence, select the operating threshold on a further validation period or through suitably separated time-aware folds, and evaluate once on a later independent period. Reliability plots, log loss or Brier score, counts per bin and uncertainty complement ranking metrics. Calibration can degrade under drift; neither weighting nor calibration guarantees transferable probabilities.

For a fixed validation set, an illustrative operating point is `τ* = argminτ FP(τ)` subject to `recall(τ) ≥ 0.85`; the project's saved threshold sweep uses this constraint and a higher-threshold tie break. This is a workload-oriented policy, not a monetary cost optimum. If no threshold meets the constraint, report infeasibility rather than inventing a compliant result. When credible costs are available, an alternative minimizes `C_FP·FP(τ) + C_FN·FN(τ)` and may also impose investigator-capacity constraints. The target and costs must be chosen before looking at final evaluation results. The project's validation-selected recall did not carry over to its later, previously exposed evaluation period; see [Chapter 5](CHAPTER_5_EMPIRICAL_RESULTS.md). Isotonic calibration is reviewed here and is not claimed as an implemented or validated project result.

## 2.8 Real-time scoring and monitoring

A useful fraud system must connect a trained artifact to a low-latency scoring path, a decision threshold, an explanation mechanism, and an audit/monitoring layer. Historical replay is a reproducible way to test ordering, latency, acknowledgement, and alert rendering when a live payment stream is unavailable. It is not equivalent to production deployment. Persistent monitoring should distinguish request volume, latency, alert rate, model version, and delayed outcome-based performance. Prediction history must retain enough provenance to identify the model, feature contract, threshold, and source of an event while respecting data minimization.

The present prototype implements FastAPI prediction/explanation routes, chronological WebSocket/SSE replay, scoped rolling state, SHAP inspection, and non-sensitive prediction-history summaries. Authentication is provided by a separate service. Production access control, external state for horizontally scaled rolling contexts, drift response, and live payment integration remain future work.

FastAPI supports HTTP endpoints and WebSocket connections [18]; a typical architecture separates request validation, feature preparation, versioned model inference, decision policy, explanation, event delivery, and monitoring. A WebSocket provides a bidirectional connection, but does not itself guarantee low latency or reliable delivery. Stateful rolling features require ordering, isolation, recovery and a shared or partitioned state design when multiple workers are used. Explanations can be measured separately or placed outside the synchronous decision path if the operational contract allows it.

For this project, P95 below 100 ms is a **proposed service-level objective**, not a universal banking standard or an achieved result. Define its boundary explicitly: for example, client-observed time for successful sequential prediction plus explanation requests, including network and preprocessing. Record P50/P95/P99, error and timeout rates, throughput, concurrency or arrival rate, warm-up, duration, hardware, model version, and whether explanations and persistence are enabled. The [load-test protocol](WEEK17_LOAD_TEST.md) specifies this boundary and notes that the full target has not been measured. The isolated TreeSHAP numbers above cannot establish compliance.

## 2.9 Synthesis and research-gap mapping

Prior work establishes the importance of non-stationarity, imbalance-aware evaluation, temporal safeguards, and explainable tree models. The implementation developed here combines those concerns in one reproducible workflow and reports their limitations explicitly. The main research gap addressed is practical integration: a chronology-aware training/evaluation pipeline is connected to versioned model artifacts, state-aware replay inference, explanations, and dashboard-level operational evidence without claiming that a public anonymized dataset represents all real-world CNP fraud.

| Theme and evidence | Gap or unresolved question | Project response and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Imbalance and metrics [1, 2, 7] | Ranking alone does not define alert workload or costs; resampling can change calibration. | Compare AP, ROC-AUC and threshold metrics on natural-prevalence chronological data; actual costs and probability calibration remain unvalidated. |
| Temporal evaluation [8, 10, 11] | Random splits do not measure later-period generalization; labels may be delayed. | Chronological splits and train-only transforms; ULB covers about two days and the test period was previously exposed. |
| Ensembles and GA [13–16] | Tuning gains require equal budgets and future-period evaluation. | RF and XGBoost are evaluated; LightGBM and GA are proposed comparisons, not results. |
| Calibration and thresholds [1, 17] | Rare positives make calibration uncertain; an operating point can fail after drift. | Thresholds selected on validation; isotonic calibration and validated financial costs remain future work. |
| TreeSHAP [4] | Background choice affects explanation semantics and runtime; explanations are not causal. | Compare raw and 50-representative settings; local measurements do not establish general acceleration or individual fidelity. |
| Serving [18] | Offline model quality does not demonstrate reliable end-to-end performance. | FastAPI and chronological replay prototype; target P95 and production-scale behavior remain unverified. |

This synthesis is an initial evidence-based draft, not a claim that a full month of searches, screening, or exhaustive review has been completed.
